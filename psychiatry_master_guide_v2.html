PMDD
NOT due to abnormal hormone levels — abnormal sensitivity to normal hormone fluctuations; mediated by gene expression & epigenetic regulation. Sx ≥1 wk prior to menses, improve at onset. Tx: SSRIs (continuous dosing best).
Suicide Assessment
Suicide screen = standardized, can be given by various people. Suicide assessment = comprehensive eval done by trained clinician, estimates immediate danger, creates Tx course.
Meds with efficacy in preventing suicide: Lithium · Clozapine · Ketamine. Minimize risk factors — especially guns in home.
ECT
Electrodes applied to skull; anesthetic + neuromuscular blockade; seizure evoked (30-60 sec); Tx 3x/wk, 6-12 Tx for remission. Side effects: retrograde amnesia (day of), headache. Indicated for severe/refractory depression, bipolar depression, mania, catatonia, NMS, and status epilepticus. 70% of patients respond favorably.
Universal Mood Disorder Exclusion Criteria
All mood disorder diagnoses require: (1) Episode not attributable to substances or another medical condition · (2) Symptoms cause clinically significant distress or impairment in functioning · (3) Symptoms not better explained by another psychiatric diagnosis.
Bipolar — Deep Notes
Suspect underlying bipolar disorder if: Family history of bipolar · Psychosis during a depressive episode · First episode younger than 25 · Hypomania that emerges with antidepressant therapy. Using antidepressants in undiagnosed bipolar can trigger Treatment Emergent Affective Switch (TEAS) — rapid switch from depressive to manic episode.
Kindling: More manic episodes → more severe they become. Triggers include: sleep deprivation, stressors, fasting, childbirth, stopping medication, and antidepressant exposure. The CLOCK gene is strongly associated with BPAD, making these patients more sensitive to circadian rhythm disturbances. Key focus during hospitalization: SLEEP.
Bipolar I vs II: Bipolar II does NOT mean "milder." These patients often have more depressive episodes, more mixed states, and more rapid cycling. The hypomanic episodes in Bipolar II cause unequivocal and observable change in functioning but are less severe — no psychosis, no hospitalization required.
MDD Synaptic Plasticity Hypothesis
Synaptic Plasticity Hypothesis (replaces outdated monoamine deficiency model): The belief that synaptic plasticity leads to effective engagement with the world. Key mediator = Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) — a chemical involved in synaptic plasticity. BDNF is increased by: ketamine, ECT, and psychotherapy. The Habenula (region of brain involved in "frustrative non-reward") is also implicated in MDD circuit dysfunction.
PMDD — Expanded
In the week leading up to menstruation, there is a 30% increase in depressive symptoms (correlates to when progesterone levels). 5+ symptoms required including: marked affective lability, irritability, depressed mood, anxiety, decreased interest, poor concentration, lethargy, appetite/sleep changes, and physical symptoms. Seems to be related to genetic predisposition and neural vulnerability → altered mRNA expression → clinical symptomology. SSRIs in luteal phase are the standard treatment.

Antidepressants
SSRIs · SNRIs · TCAs · MAOIs · Atypicals · Mood Stabilizers
▼
Why Delayed Onset?
5HT levels rise immediately (↓ reuptake) BUT autoreceptors initially fight the drug → autoreceptor eventually desensitizes (weeks) → clinical improvement follows receptor adaptation (6-8 wks).
SSRIs — Most Widely Prescribed
Fluoxetine, fluvoxamine, paroxetine, sertraline, escitalopram, citalopram. Fewer side effects, lower overdose risk. Take 4-6 wks to show effects.
Side effects: GI (nausea/diarrhea, usually limited time) · Sexual dysfx (↓ libido, anorgasmia, ED) · Serotonin syndrome w/ MAOIs · Prolonged QT · SIADH · Induction of mania · Sleep/weight changes.
MAOIs — Rare but Powerful
Classic MAOIs = irreversible & nonselective. Can't restore enzyme activity w/o synthesizing new enzyme. Wine & Cheese interaction: tyramine → ↑ circulating NE → HTN crisis. Must wait 6 wks after stopping to start new drug.
RIMAs (reversible, selective inhibitors of MAO-A): moclobemide, befloxatone — can be displaced by NE released from tyramine → MAO can break down extra NE → safer.
TCAs — "The Old Workhorses"
Block 5HT & NE reuptake pumps. Mid-level selectivity. Blocks muscarinic/H1/α1 receptors + Na+ channels.
Side effects: Anti-H1: sedation/weight gain · Anticholinergic: dry mouth/blurry vision/constipation/urinary retention/tachycardia · Anti-α1: orthostatic HTN/reflex tachycardia · Cardiac effects. For OCD: clomipramine only.
Atypical Antidepressants
Bupropion (Wellbutrin/Zyban)
  • NE & DA reuptake inhibitor
  • Lacks serotonergic component → no sexual side effects
  • Useful for pts w/ sexual side effects from SSRIs
Venlafaxine (3rd gen heterocyclic)
  • Combines SSRI + SNRI actions
  • Shares NE/5HT/DA reuptake inhibition
  • CANNOT use if had MAOIs in past 2 weeks
  • Can't stop abruptly → discontinuation syndrome

Serotonin Neurotransmission — The Biology
5HT (serotonin) is synthesized from tryptophan, transported into vesicles by VMAT (vesicular monoamine transporters), then released into the synaptic cleft. 5HT transporters (SERT) selectively reuptake serotonin, which is then degraded by MAOs. The 5HT1D autoreceptors inhibit release of serotonin. These three steps are the major drug targets for antidepressants.
Historical note: In the 1950s, Reserpine (an antihypertensive) was the first indication that 5HT, NE, and dopamine were implicated in depression. Imipramine (anti-5HT) was used for schizophrenia and elevated mood. This led to development of TCAs and MAOIs.
Why do 5HT1A autoreceptors cause delayed SSRI effect? SSRIs immediately block SERT → ↑ synaptic 5HT → autoreceptors fire back to inhibit further 5HT release. Over weeks, autoreceptors desensitize → net ↑ in serotonergic transmission → clinical improvement.
TCA NE Selectivity (Most → Least Selective for NE)
Desipramine (most NE selective) → Imipramine → Nortriptyline → Amitriptyline (least NE selective, most sedating). Clomipramine is the most 5HT selective TCA — this is why it is effective for OCD. All TCAs: can cause delirium due to strong anticholinergic activity. Overdose = lethal cardiac arrhythmias (Na+ channel blockade).

Symptoms
Positive Sx (+)

  • Disorganized speech (tangentiality, word salad)

  • Avolition (lack of motivation)

Neuroimaging & Neuropathology — Exam Favorite
Most consistent MRI finding: Enlarged lateral ventricles (ventricular enlargement) — think of it as loss of brain tissue around the ventricles. ↓ grey matter in PFC, temporal cortex, hippocampus, thalamus. NOT diagnostic on its own — used to rule out other causes and support dx.
Structural Changes
  • ↑ lateral ventricle size
  • ↓ hippocampal volume (memory deficits)
  • ↓ prefrontal cortex (negative Sx, working memory)
  • ↓ thalamic volume
  • Cortical thinning (temporal, frontal)
Functional Changes (fMRI/PET)
  • ↓ PFC activation on cognitive tasks (hypofrontality)
  • ↑ mesolimbic DA (positive Sx)
  • ↓ mesocortical DA (negative Sx)
  • Disrupted connectivity between cortical regions
Etiology
Neurodevelopmental model: Subtle defect in cerebral development disrupts late-maturing, highly evolved neocortical circuits → manifests in adult life.
Dopamine Hypothesis: Mesolimbic hyperactivity → (+) Sx · Mesocortical hypoactivity → (−) Sx · D2 receptors = most clinically relevant. Chromosome 6 harboring gene C4H = highly associated.
Poor prognosis factors: Early onset (incl. childhood), no precipitating factors, insidious onset, family Hx, poor premorbid social/sexual/work Hx, withdrawn/autistic behavior, poor support, (−) Sx, neurological signs.

EPS Timeline Mnemonic
Rule of 4s
4h · 4d · 4w · 4m
4 hours: Acute dystonia · 4 days: Akathisia (motor restlessness) · 4 weeks: Parkinsonism (bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor) · 4 months+: Tardive Dyskinesia (may be permanent)
Tardive Dyskinesia: Hyperkinetic movement disorder — repetitive involuntary purposeless movements (lip smacking, facial/tongue/limb movements). May be permanent. DA normally suppresses Ach activity; blocking DA receptors → ↑ Ach release.
Typical vs Atypical
Typical (1st gen) — D2 blockade
  • High potency: Haloperidol, Fluphenazine → more EPS, ↑ prolactin
  • Low potency: Chlorpromazine, Thioridazine → more anticholinergic, orthostatic HTN, sedation
  • Antimuscarinic action reduces EPS (balance)
Atypical (2nd gen) — D2 + 5HT2A blockade
  • Olanzapine, Risperidone, Quetiapine, Ziprasidone
  • Better for (−) Sx; more metabolic side effects
  • 5HT2A blocked → DA disinhibited → reversal of DA blockade → low/no EPS
Clozapine — Special Case
Unique atypical — greatest efficacy, most side effects. Very few EPS, no dyskinesia or ↑ prolactin. Offer to pts with persistent, clinically significant (−) Sx despite adequate Tx (non-responders).
Agranulocytosis in 0.5-2% of pts — life-threatening. Not enough WBCs from marrow. Weight gain = most significant side effect. Tx w/ Metformin → ↓ blood sugar, ↓ glucose absorption.
Aripiprazole (Abilify) — Newest Atypical
D2 partial agonist + 5HT2A antagonist. Little/no weight gain, little EPS, little sedation, little effect on (−) Sx. "Goldilocks" mechanism — activates just enough.
Cobenfyx (Newest — Muscarinic)
Xanomeline + trospium. 1st antipsychotic targeting muscarinic Ach receptors. Xanomeline = M1 agonist (CNS); trospium = M1 antagonist (periphery). Targets muscarinic pathway vs dopamine.
NMS ⚠ Life-Threatening
Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome: Idiosyncratic, life-threatening. Usually within 30 days of new medication or dose increase. Hyperthermia + autonomic instability + muscle rigidity (boardlike) + tremor/myoclonus + confusion/stupor. Tx: Immediately discontinue antipsychotic, hydration, maintain vital functions, prescribe bromocriptine + dantrolene.

Fear vs Anxiety
Fear = fight/flight — emotional response to real/perceived IMMINENT threat (from danger). Anxiety = complex cognitive/affective/behavioral/physiological response — activates to prepare for stressful events/circumstances (from thoughts). Pathological = excessive/persistent beyond developmental norms, causing clinically significant distress/impairment.

OCD
Obsessions = recurrent, persistent, intrusive thoughts/urges/images → marked anxiety. Individual tries to ignore/suppress or neutralize. Compulsions = repetitive behaviors/mental acts performed in response to obsession — aimed at preventing/reducing anxiety. Time-consuming (>1 hr/day) or cause significant distress/impairment.
OCD vs OCPD: OCD = true obsessions & compulsions, distress over lack of control (ego-dystonic). OCPD = need for control driven by preoccupation w/ order (not ego-dystonic). Tx: Psychotherapy (CBT, exposure); Meds = SSRIs (1st line), SNRIs, TCA, atypical antipsychotics. If TCA → clomipramine only.
Neurobiology of Anxiety
The amygdala alerts the brain to danger and conditions anxiety and fear. It sends signals to: (1) prefrontal cortex (organizes behaviors to cope with stress/mitigate anxiety), (2) hypothalamus (regulates sympathetic response), and (3) periaqueductal gray (modulates response). Main NTs implicated: GABA, norepinephrine, serotonin, and dopamine.
Key Definitions
Obsession vs Compulsion
  • Obsession: Recurrent, persistent, intrusive thoughts/urges/images. Individual usually attempts to ignore/suppress or neutralize via compulsion.
  • Compulsion: Repetitive behaviors or mental acts driven by obsession. Aimed at preventing/reducing anxiety or preventing dreaded event.
  • Ego Dystonic: Thoughts/impulses/behaviors in direct conflict with core values/self-image. Present in OCD but NOT in OCPD.
Fear vs Anxiety
  • Fear: Fight or flight — emotional response to a real or perceived imminent threat; stress response from immediate danger.
  • Anxiety: Complex response system that activates to prepare for stressful events or circumstances; stress response from your thoughts.
  • Anxiety Disorder: Excessive fear/anxiety/worry/avoidance behavior beyond norms — presents with cognitive, physiological, behavioral, and affective symptoms.
Notable Epidemiology
Specific phobias are the most prevalent anxiety disorder (8-12% of adults). GAD is the most common anxiety disorder in geriatric patients. GAD risk factors: history of other mood disorders, FMHx of anxiety disorders, history of sexual assault. Social anxiety typically peaks in adolescence. Agoraphobia tends to occur younger than 35 and can be chronic without treatment.
Special Subtypes
Performance-Only Social Anxiety Disorder: Fear limited to performance situations (public speaking, performing). Tx differs — use beta-blockers (propranolol for tremors/shaking) or short-term benzos 30-60 min before performance. CBT remains cornerstone.
Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety: Panic or anxiety that develops during or soon after substance intoxication or withdrawal. Treatment: stop the substance + CBT + SSRIs/SNRIs/TCAs.

Anxiety Psychopharmacology
SSRIs · SNRIs · Benzos · Buspirone · TCAs · Gabapentin
▼
Benzodiazepines
MOA: Allosteric modulators of GABA-A receptor — bind booster site (btw α & γ subunits) → enhance GABA's inhibitory effects → ↑ Cl⁻ entry → hyperpolarization → inhibit nerve impulse. Meds: diazepam, oxazepam, clonazepam, lorazepam, alprazolam.
Side effects: Sedation/drowsiness · Respiratory depression · Physical dependence · ↑ Misuse potential · Withdrawal if stopped suddenly = lethal (autonomic hyperactivity, seizures) → ↑ OD risk.
Buspirone — Unique Anxiolytic
Partial 5HT1A receptor agonist. Works both pre- & post-synaptically. Good to use because: Similar efficacy to benzos w/o dependence/addiction/sedation · No interactions · Works quicker (1-2 wk onset) · Less side effects. Side effects: dizziness, nausea, headache, rare abnormal movements. Can be used to augment SSRI or ↓ sexual side effects.
Gabapentin
GABA analog — Ca²⁺ channel modulator. Indirectly blocks Ca²⁺ channels → ↓ excitatory neuronal tone → ↓ anxiety. Side effects: sedation, dizziness, ataxia, weight gain, tolerance/dependence, withdrawal possible.
SNRIs for Anxiety
Venlafaxine, desvenlafaxine, duloxetine. 1st line for GAD (take for 12 months). CANNOT use for pts who had MAOIs in past 2 weeks. Can't stop abruptly (discontinuation syndrome: chills/dizziness/fatigue). BP elevation due to ↑ NE.

HPA Axis Dysregulation in PTSD
The HPA axis (hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal) communicates using hormones: CRH (hypothalamus) → ACTH (pituitary) → Cortisol (adrenal glands). When dysregulated due to excessive CRH release, it can lead to low cortisol levels → increased number of highly sensitive glucocorticoid receptors → overly sensitive alarm system. People with PTSD show increased amygdala activity and decreased medial prefrontal cortex activity.

Cluster Overview
Cluster A — Odd/Eccentric
  • Paranoid: Unwarranted distrust/suspicion; pervasive paranoia regarding others
  • Schizoid: Detachment, disinterest in social relationships, limited emotional range; NO cognitive/perceptual disturbances
  • Schizotypal: Intense discomfort w/ relationships; distorted cognitions/perceptions; eccentric behavior; ideas of reference, magical thinking
Cluster B — Dramatic/Erratic
  • Antisocial: Disregard for rights of others; must have conduct disorder before age 15
  • Borderline: Instability in relationships/self-image/mood; impulsivity; splitting
  • Histrionic: Excessive emotionality & attention-seeking; rapidly shifting emotions
  • Narcissistic: Grandiosity, need for admiration, lack of empathy
Cluster C — Anxious/Fearful
  • Avoidant: Avoidance of social situations involving risk of rejection/criticism/humiliation
  • Dependent: Pervasive excessive need to be taken care of; submissiveness, clinging
  • OCPD: Preoccupation w/ orderliness/perfectionism/control; no room for flexibility
Borderline PD — Deep Dive
Core traits: All-or-nothing thinking (splitting) · Suicidality · Self-injurious behavior (punishing "all bad self") · Dissociation.
Etiology: Genetics = interpersonal hypersensitivity (↑↑↑ heritability) · Environment = Hx of childhood trauma, disorganized attachment, separation problems. Brain = hypometabolic areas used to ctrl/regulate emotion; hyperactive limbic activation; ↓ volume in frontal lobe/hippocampus/amygdala.
1st line Tx = psychotherapy: Good Psychiatric Management (GPM) · Dialectical Behavioral Therapy (DBT) — mindfulness, distress tolerance, interpersonal effectiveness, emotion regulation · Mentalization-based therapy · Transference-focused · Schema-focused. Medications = adjunct (antidepressants, mood stabilizers, atypical antipsychotics).
Key DDx
BPD vs Bipolar: stressor causes rapid mood/behavior change vs sustained, less reactive mood (bipolar). PTSD: recurrent Sx associated w/ experiencing traumatic event; arousal/fear are features of both but not the defining feature of BPD. OCD: true obsessions & compulsions, distress over lack of control. OCPD: need for control driven by preoccupation w/ order (not ego-dystonic).

Alcohol & benzo withdrawal = POTENTIALLY FATAL. Must know this cold.

NOT a direct result of alcohol — chronic use + poor diet = nutritional deficiency. Prevent w/ Thiamine. Often progresses to alcohol-induced persisting amnestic disorder. Sx: nystagmus, lateral gaze paralysis, diplopia, ataxia, confusion, short-term memory impairment.

Addiction Neurobiology — Exam Favorite
D1 vs D2 Receptors: D1 = "Go" pathway (excitatory, Gs-coupled → ↑ cAMP) — motivates reward-seeking. D2 = "Stop" pathway (inhibitory, Gi-coupled → ↓ cAMP) — brakes on reward. In addiction: ↓ D2 receptors in striatum → loss of brakes → compulsive use.
Impulsive Stage (early)
  • Positive reinforcement driven
  • ↑ dopamine in nucleus accumbens
  • Drug feels rewarding → repeat use
  • D1 pathway dominates
Compulsive Stage (late)
  • Negative reinforcement driven
  • Avoiding withdrawal/dysphoria
  • Hijacked reward circuitry
  • ↑ CRF (corticotropin-releasing factor) + Dynorphin (κ-opioid agonist) → hyperkatifeia (↑ anxiety/dysphoria)
Hyperkatifeia: Dysphoric/anxious state in withdrawal = opposite of drug high. Mediated by dynorphin (κ-opioid) activation. Key driver of relapse — not just craving the high, but escaping the low.
Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder
Microcephaly, prenatal growth deficiency, short palpebral fissures. Alcohol inhibits ADH w/ EtOH → enters fetal circulation.
Sedatives/Hypnotics/Anxiolytics (Benzos)
Basically same as alcohol. Onset/duration depends on specific drug. Rarely fatal alone — mortality risk ↑ when combined w/ alcohol/barbiturates/opioids. Withdrawal: same as alcohol, later onset (1-2 wks), seizures more common, can be FATAL. Tx: benzos, barbiturates, anticonvulsants, β-blockers.
Tobacco/Nicotine
Nicotine = stimulant (sympathetic NS) → NE/Epi release → ↑ HR/BP, contractility, systemic vasoconstriction. Withdrawal: anxious, irritable, depressed, frustrated, tremors, ↑ appetite, difficulty concentrating. Tx: Chantix (varenicline), bupropion (Wellbutrin), nicotine replacement (gum/patches), psychosocial, harm reduction (e-cigs?).

Cannabis & Hallucinogens

▼
Cannabis
Active ingredient: delta-9-THC (CB1 receptor partial agonist). Concentrates up to 90% THC. Edibles: varying potency/quality; can be felt up to 4-5x stronger than inhaled; less control over titrating dose. ↑ risk of psychosis (esp. w/ genetic predisposition to schizophrenia/bipolar).
THC toxicity: Low — no reported fatal ODs; brainstem = ↓ CB1 receptor density → impossible to consume the LD₅₀. Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome: Associated w/ ↑ doses (not only w/ intoxication); hot bath/shower usually helps. Withdrawal: cravings, sleep issues, ↑ nightmares, anger/irritability/dysphoria — Tx supportive.
CBD: Non-psychoactive. ↓ reuptake/hydrolysis of anandamide (endogenous cannabinoid). 5HT1A partial agonist. Has some antipsychotic properties. Epidiolex = rare childhood epilepsies (Lennox-Gastaut, Dravet syndromes).
PCP (Phencyclidine)
Onset 10 sec-30 min. "Pain insensitivity/super strength" — often commits self-injurious acts w/o knowing. Physical: nystagmus (horizontal/vertical/rotary), HTN, tachycardia, resp depression, ataxia, muscle rigidity, seizures, coma. Psych: ↓ non time, disorganized thought, paranoia, delusions, distorted body image, combativeness. Routine acidification no longer recommended. Tx: supportive; benzos for agitation; consider antipsychotics (beware ↑ risk muscle tension/EPS/rhabdo). Withdrawal: nonspecific; rare w/ habitual use.
LSD/Psilocybin
Not generally life-threatening. Onset within 1 hr. Physical: tachycardia, diaphoresis, pupillary dilation, blurry vision, tremor. Psych: ↑ perceptions (visual distortions/illusions vs hallucinations), synesthesias, mood lability, depersonalization/derealization, paranoia, time dilation, risky behavior. Tx: supportive quiet/safe environment; agitation → benzos (mild-moderate), antipsychotics (severe).
HPPD (Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder): "Flashback" of experience after ceasing hallucinogen use. May continue for months-years. Triggers: stress, fatigue, anxiety, other drug use, dark/low stimulation environment. Intact reality testing. No Tx established.

Sleep Architecture — What % of What
N1 (Light)
  • 5% of sleep
  • Transition wake→sleep
  • Theta waves (4-8 Hz)
  • Hypnic jerks here
N2 (Intermediate)
  • 45-55% of sleep
  • Sleep spindles + K-complexes
  • Memory consolidation
  • HR/temp decrease
N3 (Deep/SWS)
  • 15-20% of sleep
  • Delta waves (<2 Hz)
  • Physically restorative
  • Growth hormone release
REM: 20-25% of adult sleep (50% in infants). First REM cycle ~90 min after sleep onset. Each subsequent cycle has more REM. EEG = looks like wakefulness. Cholinergic system drives REM; NE/5HT suppress it.
Circadian Biology — SCN & CLOCK Gene
SCN (Suprachiasmatic Nucleus): Hypothalamic "master clock." Receives light input via retinohypothalamic tract → photoreceptors containing melanopsin (ipRGCs, intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells). Light → SCN → ↓ melatonin (pineal gland). CLOCK gene: Core transcription factor of circadian rhythm. Mutations in CLOCK → disrupted sleep-wake cycles; associated w/ bipolar mania phenotype. Period gene (PER) provides negative feedback loop.
Ramelteon MOA: MT1/MT2 melatonin receptor agonist (SCN) → mimics darkness signal → shifts circadian phase → sleep onset. No abuse potential. Used for circadian rhythm disorders + insomnia. Not a CNS depressant.
Sleep Changes w/ Psych Disorders
General: ↓ slow wave sleep (N3); ↓ REM sleep latency (time to 1st REM); nighttime/early morning awakenings. Bipolar: CLOCK gene strong association; REM disruption = ↓ latency, ↑ density. Aging: ↓ REM; slightly ↓ total sleep time; sleep fragmentation w/ daytime naps; tendency for sleep phase to advance. Alcohol: ↑ slow wave sleep (N3) soon after falling asleep → overall ↓ slow wave sleep & REM → ↓ total sleep time.

Etiology Mnemonics
Common causes of delirium

Long-Term Consequences
Delirium = independent risk factor for dementia. Even after recovery, pts with delirium have ↑ risk of developing major neurocognitive disorder. Each delirium episode may accelerate neurocognitive decline. Post-discharge: delirium Sx can persist for months.
Definition & Criteria
Acute brain failure = neuropsych abnormalities. Transient, reversible dysfunction in cerebral metabolism w/ acute/subacute onset. Classified as a neurocognitive disorder. ~50% of cases missed. One of the most common psych syndromes in general hospital.
DSM-5: A) Disturbance in attention & awareness · B) Develops over short period (hrs-days), represents change from baseline, tends to fluctuate · C) Additional disturbance in cognition (memory, disorientation, language, visuospatial perception) · D) Not another neurocognitive disorder, not in context of severely reduced arousal · E) Evidence from Hx/PE/labs that it's a direct physiological consequence.

Emergency Etiologies — MUST RULE OUT ASAP
1) Wernicke's Encephalopathy (triad of 6Sx) · 2) Withdrawal (benzos, barbiturates, alcohol) · 3) Hypertensive Encephalopathy · 4) Hypoxia · 5) Hypoglycemia · 6) Hypoperfusion · 7) Intracranial bleed · 8) Meningitis/Encephalitis · 9) Poisons/Medications. Subtypes & Clinical Clues
Hyperactive: Substance-related more common. Hypoactive: Metabolic abnormality-related more common; ↑ mortality in hypoactive. Formal testing: serial 7s, spell WORLD backwards, days of wk/months of year backwards, random letter test, digit span backwards. Attention deficit = primary deficit.
Treatment
Treat underlying etiology. Nonspecific: frequent vitals, maintain fluids/nutrition, discontinue non-essential meds, address uncontrolled pain. Psychosocial: family/staff support, ambulation if possible, reassurance, supervision. Environmental: orienting cues, address visual/hearing impairment, frequent toileting. NO FDA-approved medication for prevention or Tx. Pharmacologic: Antipsychotics (haloperidol: few anticholinergic/hypotensive properties) — ONLY if verbal/nonverbal de-escalation ineffective. No benzos UNLESS withdrawal delirium. Dexmedetomidine (α2 agonist).

Addiction Neurobiology
Behavioral disorder w/ behavioral abnormalities — develop gradually & progressively during repeated exposure to a drug of abuse. Can persist for months/years after discontinuing drug. Drug-induced neural plasticity underlies change in behavior.
Reward Pathway (Mesolimbic): Core structures = medial forebrain bundle (MFB) + ventral tegmental area (VTA). Drug exposure adjusts perception of reward salience from natural → drug rewards. Stimulants/depressants/hallucinogens etc = ↑ dopamine signaling.
Addiction Components
Hijacked Reward Circuits
  • ↑ intensity of (−) emotional/motivational Sx of withdrawal
  • Results in ↑ ↑ stress
Impaired Cognitive Control
  • Basal ganglia dysfunction
  • Prefrontal cortex dysfunction
Engaged Stress Circuitry
  • Stress-sensitive brain regions: amygdala, Bed Nucleus of Stria Terminalis (BNST)
  • Aversion encoding: dynorphin
Methamphetamine (Prototype)
Potent, ↑↑↑ addictive psychostimulant. Efficient CNS penetration. Evokes release of ↑ monoamines (esp. DA) → enhanced DA in reward circuitry independent of neuronal depolarization → ↑ cAMP production → abnormal firing patterns → activation of reward-encoding neurons in nucleus accumbens.
Dopamine Balance
Too little DA: Parkinsonism · Attention deficit · Restless legs. Too much DA: Psychosis · Addiction. This is why: dopamine agonists treat Parkinson's but can cause impulse-control issues; antipsychotics block DA (treating psychosis) but cause EPS.

Learning Theory — Classical vs Operant Conditioning
Classical Conditioning — Pavlov
  • Stimulus–response learning (involuntary reflexes)
  • Pavlov's dogs: bell (neutral) → food → salivation; eventually bell alone → salivation
  • UCS (food) → UCR (salivation). CS (bell) → CR (salivation)
  • Extinction: CS repeatedly presented alone → CR fades
  • Systematic desensitization = clinical application (phobias)
Operant Conditioning — Skinner
  • Behavior shaped by consequences (voluntary behaviors)
  • Skinner's rats/pigeons: press lever → food (reinforcement)
  • Positive reinforcement: Add reward → ↑ behavior (gold star)
  • Negative reinforcement: Remove aversive stimulus → ↑ behavior (taking aspirin removes headache)
  • Positive punishment: Add aversive → ↓ behavior (scolding)
  • Negative punishment: Remove reward → ↓ behavior (lose screen time)
  • Token economy = clinical application
Key Exam Trap: Negative reinforcement ≠ punishment. Negative reinforcement INCREASES behavior by removing something unpleasant. Punishment DECREASES behavior.
Psychoanalytic Theory — Freud's Structural Model
Id
  • Entirely unconscious
  • Pleasure principle
  • Primitive drives (sex, aggression)
  • Wants immediate gratification
Ego
  • Mostly conscious
  • Reality principle
  • Mediates between Id and Superego
  • Defense mechanisms operate here
Superego
  • Partly conscious
  • Morality principle
  • Internalized values/rules
  • Generates guilt/shame
Psychodynamic Frame: Set time, place, fee, rules of interaction. Purpose = establish therapeutic alliance. Transference = pt's feelings toward therapist based on past relationships (interpret it). Countertransference = therapist's emotional reaction to pt (use it as data, don't act on it).
CBT
Problem-focused, goal-directed, active interventions using cognitive & behavioral approaches. Structured short-term (12-20 sessions). Based on principle that psych issues are partly based on: problematic/unhelpful thinking patterns · learned patterns of unhelpful behaviors · problematic core beliefs (central ideas about yourself/the world). ABCs of CBT: Antecedent → Belief (core belief) → Consequence (feeling).
Core beliefs = person's most central ideas about themselves/the world. Learned (not born w/); develop in childhood or in stressful/traumatic periods. Contradicting info ignored; not necessarily true. Rigid, long-standing; can be changed. → (−) core beliefs → dysfunctional automatic thoughts → (−) emotions/behaviors. Behavioral component: Exposure Response Prevention (ERP), Prolonged Exposure — imaginal, virtual reality, in vivo, interoceptive. Can treat: panic w/ agoraphobia, specific/social phobias, PTSD, eating disorders, OCD.
Psychodynamic Therapy
Helping bring conscious awareness to unconscious material. Subjective experience of childhood affects development & conflict formation. Behavior today due to held-on unconscious experiences from childhood. Techniques: free association, therapeutic alliance, interpretation of transference, defense analysis. 4-5×/wk for years. Indications: depression, anxiety, personality dro, trauma/stress-related dro, somatoform dro, eating dro, substance use dro.
Key concepts (Gabbard's): Much of mental life is unconscious. Transference to the therapist = key source of info. Countertransference provides window into what the pt brings out in others. Behaviors = often result of complex unconscious forces. Ego (reality), Superego (morality), Id (primitive instincts/desires).
DBT — Dialectical Behavioral Therapy
Basically CBT++. Building mindfulness, distress tolerance, interpersonal effectiveness, emotion regulation, acceptance & change. Used to Tx pts w/ emotional dysregulation, intentional self-harm, impulsive behaviors, pattern of unstable relationships. Buzzword for exams: pts w/ Borderline Personality Disorder (↑ risk of self-harm, suicidality).
Defense Mechanisms
Mature (SASH)
  • Sublimation: replacing unacceptable urge w/ socially acceptable cause of action
  • Altruis: alleviating (−) feelings via generosity that provides qualification
  • Suppression: intentionally withholding idea/feeling from conscious awareness temporarily
  • Humor: lightheartedly expressing uncomfy feelings to shift focus away from distress
Immature
  • Acting Out: coping w/ conflict using actions rather than reflections/feelings
  • Denial: avoiding awareness of reality
  • Regression: involuntarily turning back to younger behaviors under stress
  • Splitting: people are either all good or all bad at different times
  • Rationalization: asserting plausible explanations for events that occurred for other reasons
  • Projection: attribute unacceptable thoughts/behaviors to other person/s
  • Displacement: redirection of emotions/impulses to neutral other
  • Isolation of affect: separating/blocking feelings
Motivational Interviewing
Approach & techniques to ↑ motivation for change in pt. Works against clinician instinct to direct/demand change. Resolve ambivalence, move → healthier changes/habits. Change happens when pt is conscious of their own desire to change. Principles: Express empathy · Develop discrepancy · Avoid argumentation · Roll w/ resistance · Support self-efficacy. 5 Stages of Change: Precontemplation → Contemplation → Preparation → Action → Maintenance.
