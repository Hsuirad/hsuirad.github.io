Mood Disorders
MDD · Bipolar I & II · PMDD · Cyclothymia · Dysthymia
Most Likely Diagnosis
A 34-year-old woman presents with 3 weeks of depressed mood, inability to experience pleasure, hypersomnia, increased appetite with 8 lb weight gain, psychomotor retardation, fatigue, and feelings of worthlessness. She denies any prior episodes of elevated mood, decreased need for sleep, or increased goal-directed activity. Her symptoms are causing significant impairment at work. TSH and CBC are normal. What is the most likely diagnosis?
✓ Correct Answer: B
Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) — Single Episode
This patient meets full DSM-5 criteria for MDD: ≥5 symptoms (depressed mood, anhedonia, hypersomnia, ↑ appetite/weight gain, psychomotor retardation, fatigue, worthlessness) for ≥2 weeks with clinically significant impairment. Medical causes have been excluded (normal TSH, CBC). There is no history of hypomanic or manic episodes — this is critical.
Mnemonic: SIG E CAPS — Sleep ↑↓ · Interest ↓ (anhedonia) · Guilt/worthlessness · Energy ↓ · Concentration ↓ · Appetite ↑↓ · Psychomotor changes · Suicidality. Need ≥5, including depressed mood OR anhedonia, for ≥2 weeks.
⚠ Exam Trap: Choice A (Bipolar II) is a critical exclusion. You MUST ask about prior hypomanic episodes. Never diagnose MDD without first ruling out bipolar spectrum. A single missed hypomanic episode changes treatment entirely — SSRIs alone can precipitate mania in Bipolar II.
⚠ Choice C (Dysthymia): Persistent Depressive Disorder requires symptoms for ≥2 years, not 3 weeks. Also typically less severe (≥2 symptoms, not ≥5). Choice D (Adjustment) would require an identifiable stressor and would not meet full MDE criteria.
Differentiator
A 28-year-old man is brought in by his wife, who notes that 4 days ago he suddenly became extremely talkative, needed only 2 hours of sleep, started three new business ventures, and spent $12,000 on cryptocurrency. He has a history of two prior major depressive episodes treated with sertraline. He denies any psychotic features. He has never been hospitalized. Which diagnosis is most appropriate?
✓ Correct Answer: B
Bipolar II Disorder — Hypomanic Episode
Bipolar II requires: at least one hypomanic episode (≥4 days, not requiring hospitalization, no psychotic features, not severe enough to cause marked impairment) AND at least one major depressive episode. This patient's 4-day episode, with no psychosis and no hospitalization, meets hypomanic — not manic — criteria. His two prior depressive episodes complete the Bipolar II picture.
DIG FAST (manic/hypomanic Sx): Distractibility · Impulsivity/Indiscretion · Grandiosity · Flight of ideas · Activity ↑ · Sleep ↓ · Talkativeness. All present here.
⚠ Bipolar I vs II Critical Difference: Bipolar I = full manic episode (≥7 days OR hospitalization OR psychosis required). Bipolar II = hypomanic episode (≥4 days, NO hospitalization, NO psychotic features, NOT severe impairment). This patient's 4-day episode + no hospitalization + no psychosis = Bipolar II. Bipolar II is NOT "milder" — it carries the same depression burden and equal or higher suicide risk.
⚠ Action Item: STOP sertraline (SSRIs can worsen cycling and precipitate full mania in Bipolar II). Start mood stabilizer (lithium, valproate, or quetiapine). This is a common exam action step.
Drug MOA
A patient with Bipolar I Disorder is started on lithium for long-term mood stabilization. Which of the following best explains the mechanism of action of lithium that makes it particularly effective in reducing suicide risk in bipolar disorder, and what is the most important monitoring parameter?
✓ Correct Answer: B
Lithium MOA — Inositol Phosphatase + GSK-3β Inhibition
Lithium has a multifaceted MOA: (1) Inhibits inositol monophosphatase → depletes free inositol → dampens phosphoinositide signaling → ↓ excitatory neurotransmission. (2) Inhibits GSK-3β → neuroprotective effects (↑ BDNF, neurogenesis) — this may underlie its anti-suicidal effects. It also modulates adenylyl cyclase and reduces cAMP signaling.
Lithium Monitoring (narrow therapeutic index): Serum Li levels (therapeutic: 0.6–1.2 mEq/L; toxicity: >1.5) · Renal function (Li is excreted renally; NSAIDs/ACE inhibitors raise levels → toxicity) · Thyroid (hypothyroidism in ~40%) · Calcium (↑ PTH → hypercalcemia) · ECG (T-wave flattening). Withhold 48 hrs before delivery in pregnancy.
⚠ Toxicity Triad: Tremor → Ataxia → Confusion → Seizure → Death (in order of increasing toxicity). Key precipitants: dehydration, NSAIDs, thiazide diuretics (↑ Li reabsorption), ACE inhibitors.
Next Step
A 26-year-old woman with a confirmed diagnosis of MDD has been on sertraline 50 mg for 6 weeks with minimal response. She denies suicidal ideation. She has no prior hypomanic episodes. She is frustrated and wants to stop all medications. What is the most appropriate next step?
✓ Correct Answer: B
Optimize Dose Before Switching — MDD Treatment Algorithm
First step in a partial responder: Ensure adequate dose AND adequate duration. 6 weeks at 50 mg sertraline is an adequate trial at a possibly sub-therapeutic dose — the next step is to optimize (increase dose to 100 mg) before declaring failure. Adding CBT is always appropriate (combination > monotherapy).
MDD Treatment Steps: Step 1: SSRI + CBT (4–8 wks) → Step 2: Optimize dose → Step 3: Switch SSRI or add augmentation (bupropion, buspirone, atypical antipsychotic, lithium) → Step 4: Switch class (SNRI, TCA) → Step 5: MAOIs or ECT (treatment-resistant; ≥2 failed trials)
⚠ MAOIs: Reserved for treatment-resistant MDD — not first-line or second-line. Require strict dietary tyramine restriction. NEVER combine with SSRIs (serotonin syndrome). Must wait ≥2 weeks (5 weeks for fluoxetine due to long T½) after stopping SSRI before starting MAOI.
⚠ Why not lithium yet? Lithium augmentation is valid for treatment-resistant MDD but premature here — patient hasn't failed an adequate dose trial. Step before augmentation = optimize current drug.
Neuroimaging
Neuroimaging studies comparing patients with MDD to healthy controls consistently show specific structural and functional changes. A researcher presents MRI and fMRI data from a cohort of MDD patients. Which set of findings is most consistent with MDD?
✓ Correct Answer: B
MDD Neuroimaging — Hippocampal Atrophy, ↓ PFC, ↑ Habenula
Key MDD imaging findings:
↓ Hippocampal Volume: Most replicated finding. Caused by HPA axis dysregulation → excess cortisol → ↓ BDNF → impaired neurogenesis. Antidepressants (SSRIs, especially) and exercise restore hippocampal neurogenesis. The synaptic plasticity hypothesis of depression is based on this.
↓ PFC Activity (hypofrontality): Explains negative thinking, poor executive function, impaired regulation of emotion. Particularly subgenual ACC (cingulate cortex) is implicated.
↑ Habenula Activity: The habenula is the brain's "anti-reward" center — it encodes punishment and disappointment signals. Hyperactive habenula → inhibits dopamine reward neurons → anhedonia. Ketamine's rapid antidepressant effect may work partly by inhibiting habenula burst firing.
⚠ Enlarged lateral ventricles = schizophrenia. White matter hyperintensities = vascular dementia or bipolar disorder. Don't confuse.
Most Likely Diagnosis
A 30-year-old woman reports that for the past 8 months, in the week before her period, she experiences severe irritability, depressed mood, mood swings, marked anxiety, decreased interest in activities, and difficulty concentrating. Her symptoms resolve completely within 2–3 days of menstruation onset. She functions well the rest of the month. Charting confirms a consistent luteal phase pattern. What is the diagnosis, and what is the first-line treatment?
✓ Correct Answer: B
PMDD — ≥5 Symptoms, Luteal Phase, Confirmed by Prospective Charting
PMDD DSM-5: ≥5 symptoms in the final week before menstruation that improve within a few days of onset and become minimal/absent in the week post-menses. At least one symptom must be: (1) marked affective lability, (2) marked irritability/anger, (3) marked depressed mood, or (4) marked anxiety/tension. Importantly, PMDD must be confirmed by prospective daily ratings for ≥2 menstrual cycles — patient charting is required.
PMDD Treatment: SSRIs (1st line) — uniquely, can be given continuously OR luteal phase only (Day 14–onset of menses) — luteal phase dosing works because the mechanism in PMDD involves rapid modulation of neurosteroid (allopregnanolone) sensitivity, not receptor upregulation. Also: oral contraceptives (drospirenone-containing), GnRH agonists (severe cases), CBT.
⚠ PMS vs PMDD: PMS = milder, does NOT meet full DSM criteria, does NOT require ≥5 symptoms, no significant impairment. PMDD = full DSM diagnosis, marked impairment. Calcium supplementation helps PMS but is not the answer for PMDD.

Psychosis & Schizophrenia Spectrum
Schizophrenia · Schizoaffective · Brief Psychotic · Delusional D/O · Pharmacology
Differentiator
A 32-year-old man has a 3-year history of auditory hallucinations and paranoid delusions. During years 1 and 2, he also experienced a 14-month period of major depressive episode concurrent with his psychotic symptoms. For approximately 4 months of that period, his hallucinations persisted even after his depression remitted. He now presents with flat affect and social withdrawal without active psychosis. What is the most likely diagnosis?
✓ Correct Answer: C
Schizoaffective Disorder — Psychosis Present ≥2 Weeks Without Mood Episode
Schizoaffective Disorder: The hallmark is that psychotic symptoms (hallucinations or delusions) must be present for ≥2 weeks in the absence of a major mood episode during the overall illness. The mood episode (here, MDE) is present for the majority of the total illness duration. This patient's 4 months of isolated psychosis (without depression) proves the psychosis is independent of mood.
Schizophrenia
  • Mood episodes brief or absent
  • Psychosis = dominant feature
  • Mood episodes do NOT persist for majority of total illness
Schizoaffective
  • Psychosis ≥2 wks WITHOUT mood Sx (key)
  • Mood episode present for MAJORITY of total duration
  • Two subtypes: bipolar type (if manic) vs depressive type (only MDE)
⚠ MDD/Bipolar with psychotic features: Psychosis only occurs DURING mood episodes — never independent. Once the mood resolves, psychosis resolves. In this patient, psychosis persisted 4 months after depression remitted → rules out pure mood disorder with psychotic features.
Drug MOA
A patient with treatment-resistant schizophrenia who has failed two adequate trials of antipsychotics is started on clozapine. His psychiatrist explains that clozapine works differently than other antipsychotics. Which of the following best describes why clozapine has fewer extrapyramidal side effects than typical antipsychotics while maintaining antipsychotic efficacy?
✓ Correct Answer: B
Clozapine — Atypical MOA: 5HT2A + "Fast-Off" D2 Binding
Clozapine is the prototypical atypical antipsychotic. Its low EPS profile is explained by two mechanisms: (1) 5HT2A antagonism → disinhibits dopamine release in the nigrostriatal pathway → prevents the dopamine depletion that causes EPS; (2) "Fast-off" theory — clozapine has lower affinity for D2 receptors and dissociates rapidly, allowing normal dopamine neurotransmission to transiently outcompete drug binding.
Clozapine Side Effects (Must Know All): Agranulocytosis (0.5–2% → REMS program, mandatory WBC monitoring) · Weight gain (most common) · Metabolic syndrome (Metformin prescribed simultaneously) · Seizures (dose-dependent) · Hypersalivation · Myocarditis (rare but fatal) · Sedation · Orthostatic hypotension · No tardive dyskinesia · No ↑ prolactin
⚠ Clozapine is reserved for: (1) Treatment-resistant schizophrenia (failed ≥2 antipsychotics), (2) Persistent negative symptoms despite adequate treatment, (3) Schizophrenia with suicidality (only antipsychotic with anti-suicidal evidence in schizophrenia). It is NEVER first-line due to agranulocytosis risk.
Aripiprazole comparison: D2 partial agonist (not antagonist) + 5HT2A antagonist. "Goldilocks" — enough D2 activity to prevent receptor upregulation but not full agonism. Minimal weight gain, minimal EPS, minimal prolactin elevation. Used as add-on to clozapine.
Neuroimaging
A 22-year-old man with a first episode of psychosis undergoes MRI brain as part of his workup. The radiologist reports findings consistent with schizophrenia. Which structural finding is the most consistently replicated neuroimaging abnormality in schizophrenia?
✓ Correct Answer: B
Schizophrenia Imaging — Enlarged Ventricles + Grey Matter Loss
Most consistently replicated finding in schizophrenia: Enlarged lateral ventricles — present in ~70-80% of patients. This is thought to reflect reduced surrounding brain tissue (grey matter loss) rather than increased CSF production.
Full Structural Picture: ↑ Lateral ventricle volume · ↓ Grey matter in PFC (explains negative symptoms, cognitive deficits) · ↓ Temporal lobe (esp. superior temporal gyrus — Wernicke's area → may explain thought disorder/auditory hallucinations) · ↓ Hippocampal volume (memory deficits) · ↓ Thalamic volume
Functional (fMRI/PET): "Hypofrontality" = ↓ PFC activation during cognitive tasks. ↑ Mesolimbic DA → positive symptoms. ↓ Mesocortical DA → negative symptoms. Disrupted connectivity between frontal and temporal regions.
⚠ Important note: These findings are group-level statistics — no single neuroimaging finding is diagnostic of schizophrenia. MRI is ordered in first-episode psychosis to rule out organic causes (tumor, MS, autoimmune encephalitis), not to confirm schizophrenia.
Next Step
A 45-year-old man with schizophrenia is admitted to the hospital 2 weeks after his haloperidol dose was increased. He presents with temperature of 40.2°C, "lead-pipe" muscle rigidity, altered mental status, and diaphoresis. His CK is 4,800 U/L. Vitals show HR 128, BP 158/98. What is the most appropriate immediate next step?
✓ Correct Answer: C
Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS) — Psychiatric Emergency
This is NMS: tetrad of (1) Hyperthermia, (2) Muscle rigidity ("lead-pipe"), (3) Altered mental status, (4) Autonomic instability. Elevated CK confirms rhabdomyolysis from muscle rigidity. NMS is idiosyncratic and life-threatening (10-20% mortality if untreated).
NMS vs Serotonin Syndrome: Both = hyperthermia + altered MS. Key difference: NMS = bradykinesia + "lead-pipe" rigidity (slow, severe) + ↑ CK + occurs over days. Serotonin Syndrome = hyperreflexia + clonus + tremor + diarrhea + occurs within 24h of serotonergic drug. NMS → D2 blockers. SS → serotonergic agents.
NMS Treatment: (1) STOP antipsychotic immediately. (2) Supportive care: hydration, cooling, ICU monitoring. (3) Bromocriptine (DA agonist — reverses DA blockade) + Dantrolene (muscle relaxant — blocks ryanodine receptor → ↓ muscle contraction). (4) Benzos for agitation. (5) Do NOT restart antipsychotic for ≥2 weeks.
⚠ EPS vs NMS: Benztropine treats EPS (dystonia, parkinsonism from antipsychotics). In NMS, benztropine is NOT the answer — this is a medical emergency requiring drug discontinuation and bromocriptine/dantrolene.

Anxiety Disorders
GAD · Panic · OCD · Social Anxiety · Specific Phobia · Agoraphobia
Differentiator
A 35-year-old man presents with recurrent, intrusive thoughts that he has left the stove on and will cause a fire. He describes these thoughts as extremely distressing and inconsistent with his self-image as a responsible person. He checks the stove 30+ times before leaving home, which temporarily relieves his anxiety. He has tried to resist the urge but cannot. His partner has a similar need for "everything in order" and becomes angry when routines are disrupted but does not find this distressing. What diagnosis applies to the patient and what applies to the partner?
✓ Correct Answer: B
OCD (ego-dystonic) vs OCPD (ego-syntonic)
Patient = OCD: Obsessions are ego-dystonic — they feel alien, inconsistent with self-image, and cause distress. He WANTS to stop but cannot. The checking (compulsion) is performed to reduce anxiety from the obsession (intrusive thought about fire).
OCD
  • Ego-DYSTONIC (feels wrong to patient)
  • Intrusive, unwanted thoughts
  • Patient is distressed by the thoughts
  • Compulsions performed to neutralize obsessions
  • Insight present (usually)
OCPD
  • Ego-SYNTONIC (feels right to patient)
  • Preoccupation with order, control, perfection
  • Patient is NOT distressed — the WORLD frustrates THEM
  • No obsessions; no compulsions
  • Cluster C personality disorder
OCD Treatment: CBT with Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) is 1st line. For moderate-severe: add SSRI (fluvoxamine is most studied for OCD; all SSRIs are effective). Refractory: clomipramine (TCA — most serotonin-selective). Note: higher SSRI doses needed for OCD vs MDD.
⚠ OCD Neuroimaging: ↑ orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) activity + ↑ caudate nucleus activity. This cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuit hyperactivation is the basis of OCD. Deep brain stimulation targets this circuit in refractory cases.
Drug MOA
A 28-year-old woman with GAD has been offered buspirone. She asks how it works and whether she will become addicted like her sister did to alprazolam. Which of the following correctly describes buspirone's mechanism and key advantages over benzodiazepines?
✓ Correct Answer: B
Buspirone — 5HT1A Partial Agonist, No Dependence
Buspirone MOA: Partial agonist at 5HT1A receptors — acts both presynaptically (inhibits serotonin release via autoreceptors) and postsynaptically (partial stimulation). Unlike benzodiazepines, it has no effect on GABA-A receptors.
Key Advantages Over Benzos: No tolerance or physical dependence · No abuse potential · No sedation or cognitive impairment · No respiratory depression · No interaction with alcohol · Can augment SSRI efficacy and reduce SSRI-induced sexual dysfunction · Safe in elderly
Key Disadvantage: Onset is 1–2 weeks (not useful for acute panic attacks). Patients who have taken benzodiazepines may not "feel" buspirone working — important counseling point.
Benzo MOA Comparison: Benzodiazepines bind the allosteric site between α and γ subunits of GABA-A receptor → ↑ frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening (vs. barbiturates which ↑ duration). → ↑ Cl⁻ influx → hyperpolarization → ↓ neuronal firing. LETHAL in withdrawal (unlike buspirone).
Most Likely Diagnosis
A 40-year-old surgeon develops intense anxiety specifically when he must speak at grand rounds conferences, though he operates and interacts with patients and staff without anxiety. He avoids these events when possible. No other social situations cause him anxiety. He has no panic attacks at rest. What is the most appropriate diagnosis AND treatment?
✓ Correct Answer: B
SAD — Performance-Only Subtype: Beta-Blockers + CBT
The patient's anxiety is limited to performance situations (public speaking) — not general social interactions. This is the performance-only specifier of Social Anxiety Disorder.
Performance-Only SAD Treatment: Beta-blockers (propranolol — blocks peripheral adrenergic symptoms: palpitations, tremor, flushing) taken 30–60 minutes before the performance. CBT remains cornerstone (cognitive restructuring + exposure). Benzos can also be used PRN but risk sedation/cognitive impairment in performance context.
Generalized SAD Treatment: SSRIs/SNRIs for 12 months + CBT (gold standard). Performance-only ≠ generalized — the treatment differs. Propranolol is NOT indicated for generalized SAD.
⚠ Propranolol MOA: Non-selective β-adrenergic receptor antagonist → blocks NE/epinephrine effects on heart and peripheral vasculature → ↓ HR, ↓ tremor, ↓ flushing. Does NOT affect the cognitive/subjective anxiety experience — only peripheral somatic symptoms.

A 28-year-old combat veteran has a confirmed diagnosis of PTSD. He has marked avoidance, hypervigilance, flashbacks, nightmares, and negative alterations in mood. He prefers not to take medications. His friend who also has PTSD was prescribed lorazepam and suggests the veteran ask for it too. What is the most appropriate next step in management?
✓ Correct Answer: B
PTSD: Psychotherapy 1st Line; Benzos = STRONG AGAINST
The VA/DoD guidelines give STRONG FOR to trauma-focused psychotherapy (PE, CPT, EMDR) as first-line treatment. Since he prefers no medications, psychotherapy is the ideal starting point.
Benzodiazepines in PTSD = STRONG AGAINST (VA/DoD): Benzos worsen PTSD outcomes by: (1) Interfering with fear extinction (which is the mechanism of exposure therapy), (2) Worsening dissociation, (3) Worsening depression, (4) High abuse/dependence risk in trauma patients, (5) No evidence of efficacy for PTSD core symptoms. This is a heavily tested point.
VA Evidence Tiers: STRONG FOR: PE, CPT, EMDR, SSRIs (sertraline, paroxetine), SNRIs (venlafaxine). WEAK FOR: Prazosin (for nightmares — α1 blocker → ↓ NE-mediated nightmare intensity). STRONG AGAINST: Benzodiazepines, Cannabis.
⚠ Prazosin for PTSD: Only indicated for nightmares — it is NOT first-line for overall PTSD treatment. He has generalized PTSD with multiple symptom clusters, not just nightmares. CBT first.
Differentiator
A 35-year-old woman survived a serious car accident 10 days ago. Since then she has had intrusive memories, nightmares, dissociative episodes, avoidance of driving, and hyperarousal. She is significantly impaired at work. What is the most appropriate diagnosis at this time, and what is the most important prognostic fact?
✓ Correct Answer: B
Acute Stress Disorder — 3 Days to 1 Month Post-Trauma
Acute Stress Disorder (ASD): Symptoms that begin 3 days to 1 month after trauma exposure. She is 10 days out → ASD, not PTSD. If symptoms persist beyond 1 month → re-evaluate for PTSD.
ASD
  • 3 days – 1 month post-trauma
  • Same symptom clusters as PTSD
  • Strong predictor of PTSD development
  • Tx: Trauma-focused CBT (early intervention ↓ PTSD progression)
PTSD
  • >1 month post-trauma
  • 4 clusters: intrusion, avoidance, neg. cognitions, arousal
  • With/without dissociative subtype
  • Tx: PE, CPT, EMDR, SSRIs/SNRIs
⚠ Adjustment Disorder: Requires an identifiable stressor but does NOT meet criteria for another disorder. Car accident + PTSD-level symptoms = ASD, not Adjustment. Adjustment disorder does not include the specific intrusion/dissociation cluster required here.
⚠ Early Intervention: ASD is highly predictive of PTSD (~50% progress). Trauma-focused CBT started during ASD phase significantly reduces PTSD rates. SSRIs alone are NOT first-line for ASD — CBT is.

Substance Use Disorders
Alcohol · Opioids · Stimulants · Cannabis · Withdrawal Danger
Next Step
A 52-year-old man with a 20-year history of heavy alcohol use is admitted to the hospital for an unrelated procedure. His last drink was 18 hours ago. He is now tremulous, diaphoretic, and anxious. His BP is 162/94, HR 108. He denies hallucinations. What is the most important immediate next step beyond supportive care?
✓ Correct Answer: B
Alcohol Withdrawal — Thiamine FIRST, Then Benzos
This patient is in Stage 1 alcohol withdrawal (tremor, autonomic hyperactivity, 18 hours after last drink). This can progress to Delirium Tremens (Stage 3) — which is potentially fatal.
CRITICAL: Thiamine BEFORE glucose. Giving glucose to a thiamine-deficient patient can precipitate Wernicke's Encephalopathy (triad: confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia) by consuming the last remaining thiamine in the glycolytic cycle. Always thiamine first in any alcoholic patient.
CIWA-Ar Protocol: Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol. Scores 10 items (tremor, diaphoresis, anxiety, agitation, nausea, sensory disturbances, headache, orientation). Score ≥8 → initiate benzodiazepines (lorazepam, diazepam, or chlordiazepoxide — cross-reactive with GABA-A → prevent seizures and DTs).
⚠ Alcohol Withdrawal Stages: Stage 1 (0–24h): tremor, anxiety, autonomic hyperactivity. Stage 2 (24–48h): same + hallucinations (usually auditory, reality testing intact). Stage 3 (48–72h): Delirium Tremens — confusion, vivid hallucinations, autonomic instability, potentially fatal. <1% progress to DTs but high mortality without treatment.
⚠ Disulfiram is NOT used during active withdrawal — it is an aversion therapy tool given only to abstinent, motivated patients who understand they must completely avoid alcohol. Starting disulfiram while a patient is in withdrawal is dangerous.

Most Likely Diagnosis + Next Step
A 24-year-old woman is brought to the ED by her roommate. She stopped using cocaine 2 days ago after a 3-week binge. She is sleeping 15 hours/day, hyperphagia, and has expressed wishes that she were dead. Her roommate is concerned she is suicidal. Her urine toxicology is positive for cocaine. What is the correct diagnosis and most critical management concern?
✓ Correct Answer: C
Stimulant Withdrawal "Crash" — Suicidal Ideation is REAL
Stimulant withdrawal (cocaine, meth) is NOT physically dangerous (no seizures, no DTs) but carries a critical risk: suicidal ideation. The "crash" from dopamine depletion causes profound dysphoria, hypersomnia, hyperphagia, and anhedonia — identical in presentation to severe MDD.
Exam Critical Point: Stimulant withdrawal suicide risk is real. Do not dismiss as "just the drugs." Perform a full safety assessment. The patient may need inpatient admission if suicidal ideation is active with intent/plan. This is perhaps the highest-yield exam point about stimulant withdrawal.
Management: Supportive care (sleep, food, monitoring). Safety assessment. Do NOT start antidepressant acutely — if depressive symptoms persist 2–4 weeks after full abstinence, then consider antidepressant (underlying MDD vs substance-induced). No FDA-approved pharmacotherapy for cocaine/meth use disorder.
⚠ Do NOT compare to opioid withdrawal: Opioid withdrawal is miserable but not life-threatening (except neonates/severely ill). Stimulant withdrawal is NOT miserable in the same somatic way — but the psychiatric risk (suicide) makes it equally important to manage.

Sleep Disorders
Insomnia · Narcolepsy · OSA · REM Behavior Disorder · RLS · Circadian
Differentiator
A 45-year-old man presents with excessive daytime sleepiness, episodes in which he suddenly falls to the floor laughing, vivid hallucinations as he falls asleep, and brief inability to move when waking. His polysomnography shows sleep-onset REM within 8 minutes. What is the diagnosis and what is the significance of his "falling to the floor while laughing" episodes?
✓ Correct Answer: B
Narcolepsy Type 1 — Cataplexy + Low Orexin + Sleep-Onset REM
Narcolepsy Tetrad: (1) Excessive daytime sleepiness with sudden REM attacks; (2) Cataplexy (50% of cases — Type 1) — sudden bilateral loss of muscle tone triggered by strong emotion (laughter, surprise); fully conscious; (3) Hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations (vivid hallucinations at sleep onset/waking); (4) Sleep paralysis (brief inability to move upon waking).
Diagnosis: MSLT (Multiple Sleep Latency Test) — sleep latency <8 min in ≥2 naps. Narcolepsy Type 1: low/absent orexin (hypocretin) in CSF — loss of orexinergic neurons (likely autoimmune). Orexin normally promotes wakefulness and suppresses REM.
Treatment: Behavioral (scheduled daytime naps). Pharmacologic: Modafinil (Provigil — α1 agonist, promotes wakefulness; fewer side effects than traditional stimulants) — 1st line for sleepiness. Amphetamines (second-line). Sodium oxybate (GHB — improves nighttime sleep, reduces cataplexy, daytime sleepiness). SSRIs/TCAs suppress REM → reduce cataplexy. New: Pitolisant (H3 inverse agonist), Solriamfetol.
⚠ REM Sleep Behavior Disorder vs Narcolepsy: REM Behavior = acts out dreams, complex motor behaviors during REM sleep (while asleep). Narcolepsy cataplexy = brief muscle weakness while AWAKE, triggered by emotion. Completely different. RBD = associated with synucleinopathies (Parkinson's, Lewy body dementia).

Eating Disorders
Anorexia · Bulimia · BED · ARFID · Refeeding Syndrome
Next Step
A 16-year-old girl with anorexia nervosa (restricting subtype) has a BMI of 14.5 (expected 20). She has been restricting food intake for 10 months. She is admitted to an inpatient unit and a meal plan is initiated. On day 3, labs show phosphate 1.8 mg/dL (normal 2.5–4.5), potassium 2.9 mEq/L, and magnesium 1.4 mg/dL. Blood glucose is 118. What is occurring and what is the most important management step?
✓ Correct Answer: B
Refeeding Syndrome — Hypophosphatemia is the Danger
Refeeding Syndrome occurs when carbohydrates are reintroduced after prolonged starvation → insulin surge → massive intracellular uptake of phosphate, potassium, and magnesium → life-threatening electrolyte shifts.
Critical Electrolytes in Refeeding: ↓ Phosphate (most dangerous — causes cardiac arrhythmia, rhabdomyolysis, respiratory failure, hemolysis) · ↓ Potassium · ↓ Magnesium · ↓ Thiamine (B1) → Wernicke's · ↑ Blood glucose (insulin response). The hypophosphatemia here (1.8) is the red flag.
Management: SLOW caloric introduction (start at 10–20 kcal/kg/day, increase gradually over days). Aggressive electrolyte monitoring and replacement. IV thiamine (B1) before initiating feeding. Target: phosphate >2.0 before advancing calories. Cardiac monitoring.
⚠ Anorexia treatment: Family-Based Treatment (FBT/Maudsley) is gold standard in adolescents. Avoid bupropion (↑ seizure threshold in malnourished patients) and TCAs. Olanzapine (off-label) may be used for weight gain in severe cases. No FDA-approved medication for anorexia nervosa. 5.6% mortality per decade — highest of any psychiatric illness.

Most Likely Diagnosis
A 32-year-old woman presents with sudden onset left leg paralysis after witnessing a traumatic accident. Neurological exam: no reflexes or sensation changes. When asked to raise her left leg, hip extension is absent in that limb — but when the examiner places a hand under her right heel and asks her to press down, strong downward force is felt. MRI brain and spine are normal. She seems surprisingly calm about her paralysis. What is the diagnosis and what clinical signs support it?
✓ Correct Answer: B
Conversion Disorder — Hoover's Sign + La Belle Indifférence
Conversion Disorder (FND): ≥1 symptom of altered voluntary motor or sensory function, with clinical evidence of incompatibility between the symptom and known neurological disease. Normal neuroimaging confirms no structural cause.
Hoover's Sign (Positive = Functional Weakness): When the patient voluntarily tries to raise the weak leg, the contralateral hip extension (involuntary) should fire reflexively. If it does NOT fire voluntarily but DOES fire when the examiner tests the contralateral leg → "inconsistency" = functional weakness. The leg that "can't move" suddenly cooperates when the patient's attention is diverted.
La Belle Indifférence: Surprising lack of emotional concern about a potentially devastating neurological symptom. While not pathognomonic (some MS and CVA patients also show it), combined with Hoover's sign and normal imaging, it strongly suggests FND.
⚠ Conversion vs Malingering: Conversion = UNCONSCIOUS. Patient is not "faking" — symptoms are real to them. Malingering = CONSCIOUS faking for external gain. Confrontation is never appropriate in conversion disorder — it damages the therapeutic relationship and worsens outcomes. Treat with PT/OT, CBT, and avoiding reinforcement of the sick role.
⚠ Conversion vs Factitious: Factitious = conscious symptom production for internal psychological gain (attention, sick role). No external incentive (no money/legal benefit). Conversion = unconscious.

Learning Theory — Three Conditioning Principles
Token Economy
  • Positive reinforcement (operant/Skinner)
  • ADD a reward (token → screen time) → ↑ desired behavior (no self-injury)
  • NOT negative reinforcement (nothing aversive is removed)
Dog Cowering
  • Classical conditioning (Pavlov)
  • Neutral stimulus (raised voice) → paired with UCS (shouting) → UCR (fear)
  • Raised voice alone (CS) now → conditioned fear response (CR)
  • Involuntary, reflexive response
Observational Learning (Bandura): Learning by watching others — social cognitive learning theory. Bobo doll experiment: children imitated adult violence. No direct reinforcement required. Important for ARFID (food avoidance by watching parent's disgust) and substance use (modeling by peers).
Negative Reinforcement ≠ Punishment (Exam Trap): Negative reinforcement = REMOVE an aversive stimulus → ↑ behavior. Example: taking ibuprofen removes headache → ↑ ibuprofen use. Positive punishment = ADD aversive stimulus → ↓ behavior. Negative punishment = REMOVE reward → ↓ behavior.

The Bipolar Trap
THE #1 MISTAKE: Diagnosing MDD without asking about prior hypomanic/manic episodes. The question will give you a patient with depression and bury the clue of a prior elevated period ("she had a 3-day period where she barely slept but felt fantastic and started many projects"). Always screen for bipolar before diagnosing MDD. If ANY prior manic/hypomanic episode exists → it's Bipolar, not MDD. SSRIs alone in Bipolar = can trigger mania.
Bipolar I vs II: Students confuse these constantly. I = full manic episode (≥7 days OR hospitalized OR psychotic features). II = hypomanic only (≥4 days, never hospitalized, never psychotic). Bipolar II is NOT milder — equal depression burden, higher suicide risk. A patient who has had hypomania + now has a manic episode requiring hospitalization = upgraded to Bipolar I.
Delusional Disorder vs Schizophrenia
Delusional Disorder: fixed delusion ≥1 month, functioning INTACT outside the delusion, NO hallucinations (or brief/minor if present), does NOT meet schizophrenia criteria. Schizophrenia: hallucinations + delusions + disorganized speech/behavior + social/occupational dysfunction + ≥6 months. The key: Delusional Disorder patients can hold a job and have a family despite their delusion.
Schizoaffective vs Schizophrenia vs Mood Disorder w/ Psychosis
Draw a timeline. Count: (1) Total illness duration, (2) How long mood episodes lasted, (3) How long psychosis existed WITHOUT mood symptoms. Schizoaffective: psychosis ≥2 weeks without mood AND mood episode present for majority of total illness. Schizophrenia: mood episodes minimal. Mood disorder with psychosis: psychosis ONLY during mood episodes, never independent.
ASD vs PTSD vs Adjustment Disorder — Time Matters
After trauma: <3 days = normal stress response (Adjustment). 3 days–1 month = ASD. >1 month = PTSD. Adjustment disorder does NOT have the specific PTSD symptom clusters (intrusive memories, dissociation, specific avoidance patterns). Adjustment = distress proportional to stressor but doesn't meet another Dx criteria.

Emergency/Urgent Steps
Postpartum Psychosis: Inpatient admission immediately (psychiatric emergency) → Ensure infant safety → Benzo × 3 nights → Antipsychotic day 4 → Lithium at 2 weeks. Counsel: 70–90% recurrence with next pregnancy → prophylactic lithium at delivery.
NMS: STOP antipsychotic immediately → Aggressive hydration + cooling + ICU → Bromocriptine (DA agonist) + Dantrolene (muscle relaxant, ryanodine receptor blocker) → No antipsychotics for ≥2 weeks.

Suicidal patient with plan/intent/means: Do not leave alone → Remove means → Inpatient admission → Risk assessment → Address underlying Dx after stabilization.

Cold knowledge of what separates close diagnoses

Mood Disorder Cousins
PairWhat Separates Them
MDD vs Bipolar DepressionHistory of ANY hypomanic/manic episode → Bipolar. Also: earlier onset, more episodes, psychotic features, family Hx, antidepressant-induced hypomania.
Bipolar I vs Bipolar IIBipolar I = FULL manic episode (≥7 days OR hospitalized OR psychotic features). Bipolar II = HYPOMANIC only (4–6 days, NO hospitalization, NO psychosis). Having a manic episode "upgrades" Bipolar II to Bipolar I — can only go one direction.
Dysthymia vs MDDDysthymia (PDD) = depressed mood most of the day, most days, for ≥2 YEARS. MDD = full criteria ≥2 WEEKS. "Double depression" = MDD superimposed on PDD.
Cyclothymia vs Bipolar IICyclothymia = hypomanic Sx + depressive Sx for ≥2 years that do NOT meet full criteria for hypomanic episode or MDE. Bipolar II = full hypomanic + full MDE.
Psychosis Cousins
PairWhat Separates Them
Schizophrenia vs Schizophreniform vs Brief PsychoticDuration: Brief = 1 day–1 month (often stress-triggered, good prognosis). Schizophreniform = 1–6 months. Schizophrenia = ≥6 months with social/occupational dysfunction.
Schizophrenia vs SchizoaffectiveSchizoaffective: (1) Psychosis ≥2 wks WITHOUT mood episode, AND (2) Mood episode for MAJORITY of illness duration. Timeline analysis required.
Delusional Disorder vs SchizophreniaDelusional: intact functioning outside delusion, NO hallucinations (or very brief/minor), ≥1 month non-bizarre delusion. Schizophrenia: hallucinations + disorganized Sx + occupational dysfunction.
Anxiety Cousins
PairWhat Separates Them
OCD vs OCPDOCD = ego-DYSTONIC (patient suffers from/wants to stop obsessions). OCPD = ego-SYNTONIC (patient thinks their perfectionism is correct, others are the problem). OCD has true obsessions + compulsions; OCPD does not.
GAD vs MDDGAD core = excessive WORRY ≥6 months. MDD core = depressed mood/anhedonia ≥2 weeks. Can coexist. Distinguish by what came first and which is dominant.
Social Anxiety vs Avoidant PDSocial Anxiety Disorder = anxiety in specific social situations → functional impairment. Avoidant PD = pervasive pattern across all contexts, present since early adulthood, affects identity. Many consider them on a spectrum.
Trauma Cousins
PairWhat Separates Them
ASD vs PTSDASD = 3 days–1 month. PTSD = >1 month. Same symptom clusters (plus ASD requires dissociative symptoms more prominently).
Conversion Disorder vs Malingering vs FactitiousConversion = UNCONSCIOUS (patient is not faking). Factitious = CONSCIOUS, internal gain (attention/sick role, no external benefit). Malingering = CONSCIOUS, EXTERNAL gain (money, disability, avoid jail). "Real → Factitious → Malingering" = unconscious → conscious internal → conscious external.
Dementia Cousins
PairWhat Separates Them
Alzheimer's vs Lewy BodyAD: gradual memory loss first. MRI: hippocampal atrophy. LBD: fluctuating cognition + vivid visual hallucinations + parkinsonism + REM behavior disorder. DaTscan abnormal in LBD. Critical: AVOID antipsychotics in LBD.
Lewy Body vs Parkinson's DementiaTiming: LBD = cognitive/psych Sx within 1 year of parkinsonism. PDD = parkinsonism established ≥1 year before dementia. Both have Lewy bodies, both treated with rivastigmine.
FTD vs Alzheimer'sFTD: PERSONALITY/BEHAVIORAL change first (disinhibition, apathy) before memory. Younger onset (50s–60s). MRI: frontal atrophy. AD: MEMORY first, older onset. AChE inhibitors NOT helpful in FTD (may worsen behavioral symptoms).
Delirium vs DementiaDelirium: ACUTE onset, FLUCTUATING course, ↓ consciousness/attention. Dementia: INSIDIOUS onset, STABLE day-to-day (early), consciousness preserved. Delirium until proven otherwise. Patients with dementia are highest risk for delirium.
Major NCD vs Minor NCDMajor: SIGNIFICANT decline, INTERFERES with INDEPENDENCE (needs help with ADLs). Minor: MODEST decline, DOES NOT interfere with independence (can compensate).
Substance Cousins — Critical Withdrawal Facts
LETHAL Withdrawal: Alcohol · Benzodiazepines · Barbiturates. These must be treated aggressively with cross-tolerant agents (benzos).
Miserable but NOT Lethal: Opioids (unless neonate/severely debilitated). Cannabis. Stimulants. Hallucinogens.
Highest Suicide Risk in Withdrawal: Stimulants (cocaine, meth "crash"). Opioids also carry some risk. Never dismiss SI during substance withdrawal as "just the drugs."

The "Never Do" List — Exam Gotchas
Actions that are always wrong and commonly tested
▼
Drug Combinations That Are Lethal or Contraindicated
SSRI + MAOI = Serotonin Syndrome. Wait ≥2 weeks between. Wait ≥5 weeks after fluoxetine (long T½). Symptoms: hyperthermia, muscle rigidity (clonus), tachycardia, AMS, diarrhea. Tx: cyproheptadine + cooling + benzos + stop both drugs.
MAOI + Tyramine foods = Hypertensive Crisis. Aged cheese, wine, beer, fermented foods, processed meats. Mechanism: tyramine normally metabolized by MAO in gut/liver → with MAOI inhibition → tyramine absorbed → triggers NE release → severe HTN.
PDE-5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil) + Nitrates = Severe Hypotension. Both cause vasodilation — combined effect can be fatal. Absolute contraindication.
Buprenorphine given too early in OUD = Precipitated Withdrawal. Must wait until COWS ≥8–12 (sufficient spontaneous withdrawal = most receptors occupied by own opioid, not exogenous). Buprenorphine's high affinity displaces full agonist → partial activation = net withdrawal.
Antipsychotics in Lewy Body Dementia = potentially fatal. Severe neuroleptic sensitivity reaction. Use only quetiapine or clozapine if absolutely needed, with extreme caution.
Bupropion in eating disorders (especially AN/bulimia) = ↑ seizure risk. Bupropion lowers seizure threshold. Electrolyte abnormalities from purging further increase seizure risk.
Glucose before Thiamine in alcoholic patient = Wernicke's precipitated. ALWAYS give IV thiamine FIRST in any patient with known or suspected alcohol use disorder before any glucose-containing fluids.
Benzos for PTSD = VA STRONG AGAINST. Interfere with fear extinction. Worsen dissociation. Worsen depression. Create dependence in trauma population. Never use as monotherapy or routine treatment for PTSD.
Valproate in pregnancy = neural tube defects + neurodevelopmental impairment. This is the most teratogenic psychotropic. Switch to atypical antipsychotic or lamotrigine before conception if possible. Neural tube closes at week 6 — damage may occur before pregnancy is known.
SSRI monotherapy for Bipolar Depression = risk of triggering mania/rapid cycling. Always pair with a mood stabilizer. Better options for bipolar depression: quetiapine, lurasidone (FDA-approved for bipolar depression), lamotrigine, or lithium.
Critical Safety Rules
Order of operations for alcohol withdrawal: IV thiamine → CIWA-guided benzos → Electrolytes → Fluids. NOT Haloperidol alone. NOT disulfiram. NOT naltrexone. Benzos are the treatment.
When to always hospitalize: Suicidal ideation with plan + means + intent. Postpartum psychosis. Active manic episode with danger to self/others. Anorexia <75–80% expected body weight. Active delirium with unsafe behavior. NMS.
